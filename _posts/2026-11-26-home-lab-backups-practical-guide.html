Home lab backups are the boring job that decides whether a dead disk costs you an afternoon or a year of work. I learned that the slow way, and this guide covers the routine I now trust: what to copy, how often to copy it, where the copies live, and how to prove they restore.

Most people start by copying everything. That fails twice. It fills your storage with data you can rebuild in minutes, and it hides the few files that cannot be rebuilt at all. So start by sorting your lab into two piles before you touch a single tool.

What actually needs a backup

The first pile is state you cannot recreate: databases, application configuration, encryption keys, password vault exports, and anything you typed by hand. The second pile is state you can rebuild from a script or a container image. Operating system installs, downloaded images and package caches belong in the second pile, and they should not eat your backup budget.

Write the first pile down as a plain list of paths. A short list is a feature. Every path on it should have an owner, a schedule, and a restore note. If you cannot say how you would restore a path, it is not backed up yet, it is just copied.

Containers make this easier than people expect. Keep every service definition in a repository, keep the data in named volumes or a single data directory, and your backup becomes two things: the repository, which you already push somewhere safe, and the data directory, which you snapshot on a schedule. That split is the single biggest simplification I made to my own setup.

Databases deserve special care. Copying the files of a running database can give you a backup that looks fine and refuses to start. Use the database's own dump or snapshot command, write the result to a file, and back up that file. It adds one step and removes the worst surprise in the whole process.

How often and where to keep copies

A good schedule follows how much work you can afford to lose. For configuration that changes weekly, a nightly copy is plenty. For data that changes all day, run it hourly and keep fewer old versions. Match the schedule to the pain of losing that data, not to what is convenient for the scheduler.

Keep copies in three places. One lives on the same machine for fast restores after a mistake. One lives on a different machine in the same building. One lives off site, ideally encrypted before it leaves, so a theft or a fire at home cannot take every copy at once. Encryption keys belong in your password vault and on paper, never only on the machine they protect.

Retention is the part people skip. Keep seven daily copies, four weekly copies and three monthly copies, then let older ones expire. That covers the slow mistakes, like noticing a corrupted file two weeks late, without growing forever. Check the numbers against your real storage once a quarter.

Automate the whole thing, including the alerting. A backup job that fails quietly is worse than no job, because it teaches you to trust a copy that does not exist. Send yourself one message per run that says how big the copy was and how long it took. A sudden change in either number is usually the first sign of trouble.

Use a tool that checks its own work. Modern backup tools store copies in a deduplicated, checksummed repository and can verify it on demand. Run that verification on a schedule, and treat a failed check as an outage, because that is exactly what it is.

Prove the restore

A backup you have never restored is a hope. Once a month, pick one item at random from your first pile and restore it to a scratch location. Open the file, start the database, load the page. Write down how long it took and what you had to look up. The notes become the runbook you will want at two in the morning.

Do one full rehearsal per year. Pretend the main machine is gone, build a fresh one from your repository, restore the data and bring the services up. The first time you do this you will find a missing key, an undocumented port or a service that only starts in a certain order. Finding them in a rehearsal is cheap.

Keep the restore instructions somewhere that does not depend on the thing that broke. A printed page or a note on your phone beats a document stored on the failed server. Include where the keys live, which command restores what, and who to call if the answer is your own memory.

Start small this weekend. Write the list of irreplaceable paths, add a nightly copy for them, point it at a second machine, and restore one file. That one loop, run end to end, protects more than a dozen tools you have not configured. Everything after it is refinement.

Home lab backups reward consistency over cleverness. A modest routine you actually run beats an elaborate one you abandon, and the proof of a good routine is simple: you can restore on demand, and you know how long it takes.
